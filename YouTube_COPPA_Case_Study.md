# Case Study: YouTube and the 2019 COPPA Settlement

> **Date:** September 29, 2026
> **Case:** FTC and New York Attorney General v. Google LLC and YouTube, LLC (settled September 2019)
> **Type:** Unlawful data collection (a privacy violation), not a hack or security breach. The harm came from what the platform collected and used, not from an outside attacker.

---

## 1. What Happened

The FTC and the New York Attorney General alleged that YouTube violated the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) Rule by collecting persistent identifiers (commonly cookies) from viewers of child-directed channels, without first notifying parents or obtaining their consent. According to the complaint, YouTube used those identifiers to deliver targeted ads to viewers of these channels and earned millions of dollars from doing so.

**Why COPPA applied to a general-audience platform.** COPPA covers services directed at children under 13, and services with actual knowledge that they collect data from children. The FTC's position was that individual child-directed channels are online services, and that YouTube had actual knowledge those channels were child-directed. This was the first time the FTC found a general-audience service in violation of COPPA for collecting data from a child-directed portion of its platform.

**Evidence of knowledge.** The complaint pointed to YouTube's own marketing, including pitches to toy companies. Reports of those pitches say YouTube told Mattel it was a leader in reaching children aged six to 11, and told Hasbro it was the most regularly visited website for kids. YouTube also ran a separate YouTube Kids app and a content rating system with categories for children under 13.

---

## 2. Outcome

- **Penalty:** $170 million in total, with $136 million to the FTC and $34 million to New York. At the time, the FTC's portion was the largest COPPA penalty ever obtained.
- **Required changes:** a system requiring channel owners to designate content as directed to children, notice to channel owners that child-directed content is subject to COPPA, and training for employees who work with channel owners.
- **Platform changes that followed:** YouTube introduced its "made for kids" designation and limited data collection and personalized ads on that content.
- **Vote:** the FTC authorized the action 3-2, and critics argued the penalty was small relative to the revenue involved.

**Later echoes**
- January 2026: final approval of a $30 million class settlement covering U.S. children under 13 who watched child-directed content between July 2013 and April 2020.
- December 2025: Disney agreed to a $10 million penalty for failing to label YouTube videos correctly as made for kids, showing that creator self-labeling can still fail.

---

## 3. Root Causes

1. **Business incentive over privacy.** Behavioral advertising revenue depended on tracking, including on content known to attract children.
2. **Willful blindness to audience.** The platform marketed its child audience to advertisers while treating itself as "general audience" for compliance.
3. **Compliance burden pushed to creators.** There was no reliable system to identify child-directed content until the settlement required one.
4. **Weak internal controls.** Data collection was not gated by whether the audience was likely to be children.

---

## 4. Key Lessons

**1. What you say to advertisers can be used as evidence of what you know.**
Marketing claims about children's audiences created "actual knowledge" for legal purposes. Companies cannot promote a child audience commercially and disclaim it for compliance.

**2. "General audience" is not a shield.**
A platform can be liable for the child-directed parts of its service. Compliance has to work at the level of specific content, channels, and features, not just the platform as a whole.

**3. Persistent identifiers are personal information for children.**
Cookies and device IDs are not harmless for under-13s. Tracking for advertising requires verifiable parental consent, and defaults should be protective.

**4. Do not outsource compliance to creators alone.**
Self-declaration is necessary but insufficient. The later Disney case shows the labeling system can fail, so platforms need automated detection and audits.

**5. Design for the most protective default when age is uncertain.**
If a user is likely a child, collect less, do not personalize ads, and limit features that depend on tracking.

**6. Penalties are only a partial deterrent.**
Critics argued the fine was small compared with revenue. Lasting change came from the structural requirements and from follow-on litigation, so companies should not treat fines as the cost of doing business.

**7. Regulatory risk continues long after settlement.**
The 2019 case was followed by a class action approved in 2026 and by cases involving creators, meaning reputational and legal effects last for years.

**8. Privacy is a product decision, not just a legal one.**
The most effective fixes were product changes: content designation, restricted ads, and separate experiences for kids.

---

## 5. Takeaways for Organizations

- Map where children may be present, even on general-audience services.
- Align marketing claims with compliance positions, and review both together.
- Gate tracking and personalization by likely audience age.
- Build platform-side detection of child-directed content, not just self-declaration.
- Train staff who work with creators, partners, and advertisers on children's privacy rules.
- Treat regulator guidance and enforcement (including new age-assurance laws worldwide) as inputs to product design, not afterthoughts.
